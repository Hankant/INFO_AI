\section*{Abstract}

Delegating a decision to AI separates the choice to delegate from the final action. Responsibility may follow the action to AI or remain attached to the human who authorized it. This proposal examines that question in intergroup conflict. Study 1 compares a human-only condition with a condition that permits voluntary AI delegation. Two opposing groups play a Hawk--Dove game with real bonus consequences. The study measures delegation, escalation, and subsequent responsibility judgments. Study 2 holds the conflict action and outcome constant. It varies whether a decision record attributes the final choice to the human or to AI. Participants rate human and AI responsibility separately. The analysis examines how these ratings vary across benefiting and harmed records. Together, the studies assess behavioral responses to AI delegation and responsibility judgments after a conflict outcome.

\textbf{Keywords:} AI delegation; conflict escalation; responsibility attribution; intergroup conflict; Hawk--Dove game

\section{Introduction}

A person who delegates a conflict decision to AI still chooses to delegate. AI then selects the action that affects both sides. This division raises a question about responsibility: how much should group members attribute to the human and to AI? Their judgments may also depend on whether their group benefits or suffers from the outcome.

Delegation can shift responsibility judgments. In \citet{BartlingFischbacher2012}, receivers punished an agent for an unfair allocation, reducing punishment directed at the delegator. AI delegation can also change behavior. \citet{KobisEtAl2025} found that some delegation interfaces increased dishonest behavior with real payoff consequences. In conflict settings, research has examined human judgments informed by AI analysis and escalation by language models~\citep{HorowitzLinGreenberg2022,RiveraEtAl2024}. The present study examines voluntary delegation by humans when the final action affects other group members.

Human and AI responsibility may change in different ways. Studies of joint decisions and automated driving found asymmetric changes in fault and blame ratings~\citep{ShankEtAl2019,AwadEtAl2020}. The availability of manual control can itself increase blame assigned to humans~\citep{ArnestadEtAl2024}. These findings leave room for continued human responsibility after delegation. They motivate separate ratings of the human who delegates and the AI that acts.

The proposal asks three questions. How often do people delegate a conflict decision to AI? Does access to AI delegation change escalation? How do group members judge human and AI responsibility after the outcome? Study 1 examines behavior in a group conflict with real payoffs. Study 2 compares responsibility ratings for matched decision records. The two studies provide separate behavioral and attribution tests.

\section{Theory and Hypotheses}

\subsection{Delegation and conflict behavior}

Delegation transfers authority over a choice to an agent. Here, the human decides whether to delegate before AI chooses the final action. The choice is binding once made by AI. The behavioral effect of offering delegation depends on uptake and the AI decision rule. Access to AI may also change choices among people who retain control.

The group task gives each decision consequences for other participants. Inequity aversion allows utility to depend on payoff differences~\citep{FehrSchmidt1999}. Intention-based reciprocity allows choices to depend on beliefs about another actor's intentions and alternatives~\citep{Rabin1993}. These theories motivate a setting with unequal payoffs and strategic interaction. Their structural parameters are outside the proposed analysis. In the first round, each side chooses before observing the opponent's action.

\textbf{H1.} Making AI delegation available will change the probability of conflict escalation. The direction remains open until the AI rule and pilot results are fixed.

\subsection{Responsibility after delegation}

The Triangle Model of Responsibility links prescriptions, identity, and events~\citep{SchlenkerEtAl1994}. The Path Model of Blame distinguishes causality, intentionality, obligation, and the capacity to prevent an outcome~\citep{MalleEtAl2014}. These theories give responsibility judgments several possible bases. In the present setting, AI performs the final action and the human authorizes the transfer of control.

The two roles support competing expectations. Responsibility may shift toward AI because it makes the final choice. Human responsibility may persist because the person chose to delegate. Separate ratings allow both actors to receive high responsibility. They also allow a decline in human responsibility without a corresponding increase for AI.

\textbf{H2.} Holding the action and outcome constant, AI responsibility will be higher when the record attributes the final choice to AI than when it attributes that choice to the human.

The human rating determines how this result is interpreted. Higher AI responsibility with lower human responsibility indicates a transfer in ratings. Higher AI responsibility with a persistently high human rating indicates shared responsibility. A decline in the human rating alone would leave H2 unsupported. These are descriptive patterns in the joint ratings.

\subsection{Payoff position}

The culpable-control model allows evaluations of an actor and outcome to influence judgments of control and causation~\citep{Alicke2000}. \citet{DongBocian2024} found that self-interest and group relationships affect responsibility attributed to AI. Their comparisons concerned self versus other and ingroup versus outgroup relations. The present study examines benefiting and harmed positions in a conflict outcome.

\textbf{H3.} Benefiting and harmed positions will be associated with different human--AI responsibility-rating profiles. The hypothesis leaves the direction open. It concerns the payoff-position by rated-target interaction, $P\times Q$. Whether final decision-maker further modifies this difference is a separate $F\times P\times Q$ comparison. The primary and supplementary status of these contrasts will be fixed before analysis.

\section{Study 1: Delegation and Escalation}

\subsection{Task and conditions}

Two opposing three-person groups form an experimental unit. Each group has one decision-maker and two members without decision authority. The decision-maker selects the group's action. All three members share its payoff outcome. The groups choose simultaneously and receive no information about the opponent's current choice.

Decision-makers are randomly assigned to \Baseline{} or \AID{}. In C0, the human chooses the final action. In C1, the human can retain control or delegate to AI. The human and AI receive the same task information. Delegation occurs before the AI action is shown. The human cannot preview, modify, veto, withdraw, or reconfirm that action. The opponent sees neither the delegation condition nor the identity of the final decision-maker before choosing.

The conflict task uses the Hawk--Dove payoff structure in Table~\ref{tab:hawk-dove}. Hawk denotes escalation; Dove denotes restraint. The resource has value $V$, and mutual escalation has cost $C$, with $C>V>0$~\citep{MaynardSmithPrice1973}.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Standard Hawk--Dove payoff structure}
\label{tab:hawk-dove}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
\textbf{Focal group choice} & \textbf{Opponent chooses Dove} & \textbf{Opponent chooses Hawk}\\
\midrule
Dove & $(V/2,V/2)$ & $(0,V)$\\
Hawk & $(V,0)$ & $((V-C)/2,(V-C)/2)$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Unilateral escalation can benefit one group at the other's expense. Mutual escalation is costly to both groups. Both actions jointly determine the outcome. Exact bonus amounts and the within-group allocation rule remain to be fixed through pilot testing.

\subsection{Procedure and measures}

Participants receive the payoff rules and complete comprehension checks. They are randomly allocated to groups, and a decision-maker is randomly selected in each group. Members do not communicate during the task. C0 decision-makers choose Hawk or Dove. C1 decision-makers first choose whether to delegate. Both final actions are implemented together, and the outcomes affect participants' real bonuses.

Participants then see their own group's authorization record, both final actions, and the payoff outcome. Members rate responsibility for their group's final choice. C0 collects a human rating only. C1 collects separate human and AI ratings. A C1 record states when the human declined delegation. The decision-maker's self-rating and AI rating are analyzed separately from members' evaluations.

The human item asks: ``For the final conflict choice made by your group, how much responsibility should the group's human decision-maker bear?'' The AI item replaces ``human decision-maker'' with ``AI decision agent.'' Each response ranges from 0 (no responsibility at all) to 100 (full responsibility). The two ratings are independent, with randomized question order and no fixed total. AI ratings are absent in C0 and remain missing in analysis.

Separate evaluation of human and automated actors follows prior work on fault, blame, and responsibility~\citep{ShankEtAl2019,AwadEtAl2020,DongBocian2024}. The present items are adapted responsibility judgments. They measure neither punishment nor legal liability.

Payoff position is recorded for each evaluator from the group's shared outcome. The reference payoff and inclusion rules for benefiting and harmed records remain to be specified. Mutual escalation and mutual restraint will be reported separately. Members of the same group share an outcome and must be treated as dependent observations.

The first round provides the primary comparison. Any second round will examine how previous outcomes and AI experience relate to later delegation. Its results will remain separate from the first-round test.

\subsection{Analysis}

The analysis reports delegation within C1 and compares final escalation between C0 and C1. Equation~\ref{eq:study_1} gives the primary model:

\begin{equation}
E_{gs}=\alpha_1+\beta_TT_{gs}+u_s+\varepsilon_{gs}.
\label{eq:study_1}
\end{equation}

$E_{gs}$ is final escalation by group $g$ in session $s$. $T_{gs}$ indicates access to AI delegation, and $u_s$ represents session variation. The coefficient $\beta_T$ estimates the effect of offering delegation. Comparisons between people who accept and decline delegation remain descriptive because that choice is voluntary. Standard errors will account for dependence within conflict pairs or sessions.

Human responsibility ratings will also be compared between C0 and C1. Within C1, joint ratings will be described by actual delegation, payoff position, and rated target. These analyses will account for evaluator, group, and session dependence. Mutual escalation, mutual restraint, and asymmetric action profiles will be reported as supplementary outcomes.

Participants will provide informed consent before the task. Ethics approval or an exemption determination will precede recruitment. Base compensation and bonuses will be separated, with no negative total payment. The ethics application and debriefing will disclose any pregenerated AI output or simulated opponents. Pilot testing will assess task comprehension, delegation uptake, AI escalation, and the frequency of asymmetric outcomes.

\section{Study 2: Responsibility for Matched Decisions}

\subsection{Design}

Participants evaluate a completed conflict record presented as concerning their side. Matched records hold the payoff rule, both actions, and the focal outcome constant. One version states that the human retained control and made the choice. The other states that the human voluntarily delegated and AI made the same choice. Both versions state that AI delegation was available. Participants therefore complete both responsibility items described in Study 1.

The final decision-maker is randomized within benefiting and harmed record sets. This comparison tests whether attributing the same choice to the human or AI changes responsibility ratings. It also tests whether the rating difference varies with payoff position. The manipulation changes the recorded delegation and final choice together; their separate effects remain unidentified.

The source of the records and their connection to evaluators' actual bonuses remain unresolved. Until that payment link is established, Study 2 concerns assigned records. Use of participants' own Study 1 records would also require a protocol for information they have already observed. These choices will be settled before data collection.

\subsection{Analysis}

Equation~\ref{eq:study_2} models the repeated responsibility ratings:

\begin{equation}
R_{igt}=\alpha_2+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{X}_{igt}+u_i+\varepsilon_{igt}.
\label{eq:study_2}
\end{equation}

$R_{igt}$ is evaluator $i$'s rating of target $t$ in record $g$. The vector $\mathbf{X}_{igt}$ contains final decision-maker $F$, payoff position $P$, rated target $Q$, and their two-way and three-way interactions. The term $u_i$ represents evaluator variation. The $F\times Q$ interaction tests whether the manipulation affects human and AI ratings differently. The $P\times Q$ interaction addresses H3. The $F\times P\times Q$ interaction tests whether the manipulation's effect on the rating profile varies by payoff position.

Joint marginal means and confidence intervals will describe the human and AI ratings. Claims of little change will use an equivalence test or a prespecified smallest effect of interest. The sum of the two ratings will not serve as a total responsibility score. Payoff-position comparisons remain conditional on the outcomes shown in the records.

Each study will have a separate simulation-based power analysis. Preregistration will specify the AI rule, primary outcomes, clustering, responsibility comparisons, and multiple-testing procedures. The designs test behavior and subsequent attribution separately. They do not estimate responsibility judgments as a mediator of earlier conflict choices.

\section{Expected Contribution}

The study examines how final action and prior authorization relate to responsibility after AI delegation. Independent ratings can show whether responsibility shifts toward AI or remains high for both actors. Continued human responsibility would be consistent with responsibility for authorizing delegation. Establishing that mechanism would require additional evidence.

The group setting also connects AI delegation to consequences for other people. Study 1 measures escalation and responsibility after real bonus outcomes. Study 2 isolates the effect of the recorded decision arrangement for a fixed action and outcome. Together, they can show how behavioral effects and responsibility judgments differ across the two comparisons. The current design supports conclusions about AI delegation in this setting. A human-agent comparison would be needed to establish an effect unique to AI.
