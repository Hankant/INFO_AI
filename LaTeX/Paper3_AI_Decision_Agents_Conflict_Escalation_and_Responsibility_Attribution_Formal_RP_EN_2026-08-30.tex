\section*{Abstract}

AI decision agents may affect conflict behavior and later judgments of responsibility. Delegation research examines whether people transfer decision authority and whether that transfer changes behavior. Research on AI responsibility examines how people evaluate humans and AI after an outcome. Research on AI and conflict asks whether AI analysis, advice, or model decisions affect the use of force and conflict escalation. The studies reviewed here cover different stages of the decision process. They do not explain within one framework what happens when a human voluntarily transfers final authority to AI and group members later judge responsibility from positions of benefit or harm.

The proposal consists of two experiments. Study 1 uses a group-based Hawk--Dove game and randomly compares two decision environments. A human must make the final conflict choice in \Baseline{}. In \AID{}, the human may retain final authority or transfer it to an AI decision agent. Delegation is binding once confirmed. The human cannot preview, modify, veto, or reconfirm the AI choice. Study 1 identifies the overall effect of making AI delegation available on final conflict escalation and records actual delegation separately. Study 2 uses matched decision records. The rules, actions, and outcome are held constant, while the recorded final decision-maker is randomized between the human and AI. Study 2 identifies the effect of the recorded delegation and final-decision arrangement on responsibility ratings.

When AI is available, participants provide two independent responsibility ratings from 0 to 100. One concerns the human decision-maker, and the other concerns the AI decision agent. The human-only baseline in Study 1 collects only the human rating. A missing AI rating in this condition is not coded as zero. The ratings need not sum to 100, so participants may hold both actors responsible. Study 1 members evaluate their own group's decision-makers after real payoff consequences. Study 2 compares benefiting and harmed records. The connection between those records and evaluators' actual bonuses remains to be specified. The main causal tests remain separate: Study 1 addresses behavior, and Study 2 addresses attribution. Post-outcome responsibility ratings are not treated as a causal mediator of earlier delegation or escalation.

\textbf{Keywords:} AI delegation; decision agents; conflict escalation; responsibility attribution; intergroup conflict; Hawk--Dove game

\section{Research Background and Problem}

\subsection{Practical background}

AI increasingly acts as a decision agent in high-stakes settings, alongside its roles in information analysis and advice. This proposal defines AI decision agency as a voluntary transfer of final authority from a human to AI. The human authorizes the transfer. AI makes the final action choice, and that choice directly produces consequences. The division between authorization and final choice may affect conflict behavior and later judgments of responsibility.

Intergroup conflict is a setting in which one person's decision affects several participants. A decision-maker's choice affects the decision-maker and other members of the same group. It also combines with the opposing group's choice to determine both groups' payoffs. The evaluators are group members who benefited or were harmed by the outcome. The decision therefore has consequences for other participants. The design also creates strategic interdependence and payoff-based evaluation positions.

\subsection{Prior research}

Delegation research shows that transferring authority to an agent may change behavior and later responsibility attribution. \citet{BartlingFischbacher2012} allowed receivers affected by an unfair allocation to evaluate decision-makers through costly punishment. They found that delegation shifted much of the punishment, and the responsibility attribution inferred from it, from the delegator to the agent. \citet{KobisEtAl2025} showed that some AI-delegation interfaces change dishonest behavior with real payoff consequences. This literature asks whether people delegate and what action follows.

Research on AI responsibility examines how people assign responsibility after an outcome. \citet{ShankEtAl2019} found that different human--AI decision structures changed the moral fault assigned to humans. The fault assigned to AI did not always change in parallel. In a shared-control setting, \citet{AwadEtAl2020} rated human drivers and automated systems separately. Their blame ratings could change asymmetrically; the scale did not force them to move in opposite directions. \citet{ArnestadEtAl2024} showed that the mere existence of a manual-control option can increase human blame for AI mistakes. \citet{DongBocian2024} found that self-interest and group relationships affect how people attribute moral responsibility to AI.

Research on AI and conflict indicates that AI involvement may change crisis judgments and escalation choices. \citet{HorowitzLinGreenberg2022} compared judgments about the use of force when intelligence was analyzed by AI or by humans. They also compared retaliation judgments after accidents involving an AI-enabled weapon or only human operators. \citet{RiveraEtAl2024} examined escalation behavior by language models in simulated military and diplomatic crises. These studies show that AI can take part in conflict processes. They do not jointly observe voluntary human delegation, real group payoffs, and later responsibility judgments about both the human and AI.

\subsection{Research gap}

The relevant literatures examine delegation, conflict behavior, and responsibility attribution separately. Delegation studies usually treat behavior as the main outcome. Studies of AI responsibility often begin with a completed scenario. Studies of AI and conflict usually measure support for force, retaliation judgments, or model escalation choices. Actual decisions unfold in sequence. A human decides whether to transfer final authority to AI. The human or AI then makes a conflict choice. Both sides' actions produce the payoff outcomes, after which group members judge responsibility.

None of the representative studies reviewed here uses one framework to identify both questions. One question is whether providing an AI decision agent changes final conflict escalation. The other is whether the recorded assignment of final authority to the human or AI changes responsibility judgments when the action and outcome are held constant. The studies also do not ask group members who benefit or are harmed to evaluate their own human and AI. We therefore do not know whether people in different payoff positions understand responsibility after AI delegation in the same way.

\section{Research Questions}

The main research question is:

\begin{quote}
In intergroup conflict, does the introduction of an AI decision agent change conflict escalation and group members' responsibility judgments of the human and AI?
\end{quote}

The main question has two parts:

\textbf{RQ1: Escalation.} Does making AI delegation available change final conflict escalation?

\textbf{RQ2: Responsibility attribution.} After the conflict outcome, how do benefiting and harmed groups evaluate the responsibility of their own human decision-maker and AI decision agent?

Actual delegation is recorded within the AI Delegation condition as a descriptive process measure. It is not an independent research question. RQ1 uses the randomized availability of AI delegation to identify an overall change in escalation. RQ2 compares independent responsibility ratings for the human and AI. Its matched-record study identifies the effect of the recorded final-decision arrangement. Decision-makers' ratings of themselves and AI are reported as a supplementary comparison. They are not pooled with the main comparison between benefiting and harmed groups. The two questions concern the same decision process. They do not form an identified causal mediation chain.

\section{Theoretical Foundations}

\subsection{Delegation and responsibility shifting}

\term{Delegation} means that a decision-maker voluntarily transfers authority over a choice to an agent. This definition excludes advice-taking, joint decision-making, and final human confirmation. Here, delegation occurs at the level of the individual decision-maker and is an observed behavioral variable. \citet{BartlingFischbacher2012} show that delegation can change later responsibility attribution. \citet{KobisEtAl2025} show that AI delegation can change behavior with material consequences.

These studies suggest two possible patterns. Responsibility may follow final authority and shift toward the agent. It may also remain with the human who authorized the transfer, even after that person gives up the final action. Separate ratings for the human and AI distinguish these patterns.

\subsection{Responsibility judgments with multiple actors}

The Triangle Model of Responsibility in \citet{SchlenkerEtAl1994} relates responsibility judgments to connections among prescriptions, identity, and events. The Path Model of Blame in \citet{MalleEtAl2014} distinguishes the roles of causality, intentionality, obligation, and capacity to prevent an outcome. Responsibility judgments can therefore draw on evidence beyond final control.

In AI delegation, the human authorizes the transfer and AI makes the final action choice. Either can become a target of responsibility judgment. The proposal does not combine role obligation, decision control, and causal contribution into a new responsibility construct. It also does not estimate their separate causal effects. Existing responsibility theories organize two possible outcomes. Responsibility may follow final authority to AI, or it may remain with the human who authorized the transfer.

\subsection{Evaluator payoff position}

The culpable-control model in \citet{Alicke2000} proposes that an evaluator's general assessment of an actor and outcome can influence how the evaluator interprets control and causal evidence. \citet{DongBocian2024} apply this question to AI. They show that people attribute moral responsibility to AI differently when evaluating themselves or ingroup members. Their studies manipulate self versus other and ingroup versus outgroup relations. They do not directly test benefiting and harmed positions created by real monetary outcomes.

We define \term{evaluator payoff position} as the position of benefit or harm created by realized experimental payoffs at the time of evaluation. This outcome position is measured at the evaluator level. It is distinct from a personality trait or general group identification. In Study 1, both groups' choices produce the benefiting and harmed positions. In Study 2, the final decision-maker is randomized within each payoff position. The analysis asks whether the same change in final authority produces different responsibility judgments across payoff positions.

\subsection{Social preferences and the group design}

The inequity-aversion model in \citet{FehrSchmidt1999} allows individual utility to depend on payoff differences between self and others. The intention-based reciprocity model in \citet{Rabin1993} allows behavior to depend on beliefs about another actor's kindness, hostility, and available actions. This study does not estimate the structural parameters of either model. The models motivate the use of a group-based conflict task. When a choice affects real participants and produces unequal payoffs, behavior may reflect risk preference, concern for others' outcomes, and expectations about an opponent's likely behavior and intentions. The first round does not reveal the opponent's action before choice, so the design cannot identify reciprocity in response to an observed action.

\section{Conceptual Model and Hypotheses}

\subsection{Conceptual model}

The equation below shows the sequence of the study:

\begin{equation*}
\underbrace{T}_{\substack{\text{random availability of}\\\text{AI delegation}}}
\longrightarrow
\underbrace{D}_{\substack{\text{actual}\\\text{delegation}}}
\longrightarrow
\underbrace{E}_{\substack{\text{final}\\\text{escalation}}}
\longrightarrow
\underbrace{P}_{\substack{\text{payoff}\\\text{position}}}
\longrightarrow
\underbrace{R}_{\substack{\text{responsibility}\\\text{rating}}}.
\end{equation*}

$T$ is the randomized treatment in Study 1, and $D$ is the participant's voluntary delegation choice. $E$ is the final escalation outcome, $P$ is the benefiting or harmed payoff position, and $R$ is the responsibility rating of the human or AI. The arrows show the order of the study. They do not give every link a causal interpretation. Study 1 supports a causal interpretation of $T\rightarrow E$. Study 2 supports a causal interpretation of final decision-maker $F\rightarrow R$. Actual delegation $D$ is not randomized in Study 1.

\subsection{Hypotheses}

\textbf{H1: AI delegation availability and conflict escalation.} Relative to \Baseline{}, \AID{} will change the probability of choosing escalation. The direction depends on actual delegation uptake and the fixed AI decision rule. It may also depend on changes in the choices of participants who decline delegation. A directional prediction will be preregistered after the AI rule and pilot results are fixed.

\textbf{H2: Final decision-maker and rated target.} Holding the final action and outcome constant, an AI-made final choice will produce a higher AI responsibility rating than the same choice recorded as having been made by a human.

Three joint-rating patterns are considered. The third does not support H2. The set is not exhaustive:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Responsibility transfer:} AI responsibility increases and human responsibility decreases.
\item \textbf{Shared responsibility:} AI responsibility increases while human responsibility remains high.
\item \textbf{Responsibility not fully taken up:} human responsibility decreases without a clear increase in AI responsibility.
\end{itemize}

These labels describe how the two responsibility ratings move together. They are descriptive labels, not established psychological constructs. They do not assume a fixed total amount of responsibility.

\textbf{H3: Interaction between payoff position and rated target.} Benefiting and harmed groups will form different human--AI responsibility-rating profiles. Existing evidence does not show which group will assign more responsibility to AI. The hypothesis concerns $P\times Q$, which captures differences in rating profiles by payoff position. A separate $F\times P\times Q$ contrast tests whether the effect of the recorded final-decision arrangement on the two ratings differs by position. The preregistration will specify which comparison is primary and which is supplementary. Neither comparison has a directional prediction.

\section{Research Design}

\subsection{Study 1: Voluntary AI delegation in group conflict}

Each experimental unit contains two opposing three-person groups. Each group consists of one decision-maker and two members without decision authority. The decision-maker's choice determines the group's action, and all three members share the group's payoff outcome. The two groups choose simultaneously. Neither side can observe the opponent's current action before making its own choice.

Decision-makers are randomly assigned to one of two conditions:

\begin{itemize}
\item \Baseline{}: the human must make the final choice.
\item \AID{}: an AI decision agent is available, and the human may retain final authority or voluntarily implement pure delegation.
\end{itemize}

Four restrictions define pure delegation. The AI and human receive the same task information. Delegation occurs before the final action. Once generated, the AI choice is implemented directly. The human cannot preview, modify, veto, withdraw, or reconfirm the AI choice. At the time of choice, the opponent does not know whether AI delegation is available to the focal group or who makes its final action.

The randomized independent variable in Study 1 is the availability of AI delegation. Actual delegation is chosen by the participant. It is recorded as a descriptive process measure and used as a conditional variable in later analysis. Differences between delegators and non-delegators cannot be interpreted as the causal effect of delegation itself.

\subsection{Group-based Hawk--Dove game}

The conflict task is a Hawk--Dove game. Hawk represents escalation and Dove represents restraint. The standard payoff structure is shown in Table~\ref{tab:hawk-dove}, where $V$ is the value of the contested resource and $C$ is the cost of mutual escalation, with $C>V>0$~\citep{MaynardSmithPrice1973}.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Standard Hawk--Dove payoff structure}
\label{tab:hawk-dove}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
\textbf{Focal group choice} & \textbf{Opponent chooses Dove} & \textbf{Opponent chooses Hawk}\\
\midrule
Dove & $(V/2,V/2)$ & $(0,V)$\\
Hawk & $(V,0)$ & $((V-C)/2,(V-C)/2)$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The task has three features relevant to the proposal. Unilateral escalation may yield a higher payoff and harm the other group. Mutual escalation imposes a cost on both groups. The two groups' actions jointly determine payoffs, so a decision-maker cannot know the group's realized payoff position before choosing. Exact monetary values and the within-group allocation rule will be fixed after pilot testing and preregistered.

\subsection{Study 1 procedure}

\begin{enumerate}
\item Participants complete informed consent, role instructions, and comprehension checks.
\item Participants are randomly assigned to two three-person groups, and one decision-maker is randomly selected in each group.
\item All participants receive the payoff rules, information boundaries, and decision-authority rules. Group members do not communicate during the decision task.
\item Decision-makers are randomly assigned to C0 or C1.
\item C0 decision-makers choose Hawk or Dove directly. C1 decision-makers either retain control or delegate to AI.
\item Both groups' final actions are implemented simultaneously, and group payoffs are realized.
\item Participants observe their own authorization record, both final actions, and the payoff outcome.
\item In C0, members rate only their human decision-maker. In C1, they rate the human and AI separately; when delegation is declined, the record states that AI did not make the final choice. Decision-makers provide corresponding self-ratings separately. Missing AI ratings in C0 are not coded as zero.
\item Participants complete final comprehension checks and supplementary measures.
\end{enumerate}

The first-round between-subject comparison is the primary test. If a second round is added, it will only explore whether the previous payoff and AI experience change later delegation intentions or choices. Its results will not be pooled with the first-round result.

\subsection{Study 2: Responsibility ratings for matched records}

Study 2 identifies the effect of the recorded delegation and final-decision arrangement on responsibility ratings. Participants read a completed conflict record that is described as involving their side. The source of these records has not yet been specified. The protocol also needs to specify whether and how the record's payoffs affect evaluators' actual bonuses. A benefiting or harmed role described in a record does not create a real financial position by itself. Until the payment link is implemented, Study 2 claims will be limited to evaluations of assigned records. If participants' own Study 1 records are used, the protocol must explain how the randomized record information relates to events they have already observed. The payoff rule, focal action, opponent action, and focal outcome are held constant. Only the recorded final decision-maker is randomized:

\begin{itemize}
\item the human retained final authority and personally made the choice; or
\item the human voluntarily delegated and AI made the same final choice.
\end{itemize}

Both versions state that AI delegation had been available. Participants therefore rate the human decision-maker and the AI decision agent in every condition. Separate matched sets are constructed for benefiting and harmed records. The final decision-maker is randomized within each payoff position. Study 2 can test whether the same action and outcome receive different human and AI responsibility ratings when AI makes the final choice. It also tests whether this effect differs by payoff position.

\subsection{Measurement of responsibility}

Responsibility is the evaluator's judgment of how much each actor should be held responsible for the group's final conflict choice. The rating does not measure legal liability, compensation, organizational sanctions, or experimental punishment.

In Study 1, C0 participants receive only the human item; C1 participants receive both items. Both Study 2 conditions describe AI delegation as available and include both items. The questions are:

\begin{quote}
For the final conflict choice made by your group, how much responsibility should the group's human decision-maker bear?
\end{quote}

\begin{quote}
For the final conflict choice made by your group, how much responsibility should the group's AI decision agent bear?
\end{quote}

Both ratings range from 0 (no responsibility at all) to 100 (full responsibility). Question order is randomized. The ratings are independent and need not sum to 100. \citet{AwadEtAl2020} separately assessed blame assigned to humans and automated systems in shared control. \citet{ShankEtAl2019} separately assessed the moral fault of humans and AI. \citet{DongBocian2024} measured moral responsibility attributed to AI. These studies support treating the human and AI as separate targets of evaluation. The responsibility items used here do not reproduce any one of these measures exactly.

Payoff position comes from the group's shared outcome and is recorded for each evaluator. Members of the same group do not constitute independent assignments to a payoff condition. Before confirmatory analysis, the protocol will fix the reference payoff, the records included in the primary benefit--harm comparison, and the treatment of mutual escalation and mutual restraint. These symmetric outcomes will be reported separately. They will not be pooled under an undefined ``joint outcome.''

\section{Analysis and Causal Identification}

\subsection{Study 1: Delegation and escalation}

Actual delegation is reported only within C1, including its overall rate, confidence interval, and associations with preregistered measures. Because delegation is a voluntary choice, comparisons between delegators and non-delegators are descriptive and have no causal interpretation.

Equation~\ref{eq:study_1} gives the primary Study 1 model:

\begin{equation}
E_{gs}=\alpha_1+\beta_TT_{gs}+u_s+\varepsilon_{gs}.
\label{eq:study_1}
\end{equation}

$E_{gs}$ is the final escalation outcome of group $g$ in experimental session $s$. $T_{gs}$ indicates whether AI delegation is available, and $u_s$ captures session-level variation. The main parameter, $\beta_T$, is the intention-to-treat effect of making AI delegation available on final escalation. It is not the causal effect of actual delegation.

Because both groups' actions jointly determine the conflict outcome, standard errors will be clustered at least at the conflict-pair or experimental-session level. Mutual escalation, mutual restraint, and asymmetric action profiles are reported as pair-level supplementary outcomes.

Study 1 also compares human responsibility ratings between C0 and C1. Within C1, the analysis describes human and AI ratings in relation to actual delegation, group payoff position, and rated target. These conditional associations do not identify the independent causal effects of delegation or payoff position. The analysis will account for dependence at the evaluator, group, and session levels. Missing AI ratings in C0 will not be replaced by zeros.

\subsection{Study 2: Responsibility ratings}

Equation~\ref{eq:study_2} gives the primary Study 2 model:

\begin{equation}
R_{igt}=\alpha_2+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{X}_{igt}+u_i+\varepsilon_{igt}.
\label{eq:study_2}
\end{equation}

$R_{igt}$ is evaluator $i$'s responsibility rating of target $t$ in record $g$. The vector $\mathbf{X}_{igt}$ contains final decision-maker $F$, payoff position $P$, rated target $Q$, all two-way interactions, and the three-way interaction. The $F\times Q$ interaction tests whether the recorded arrangement has different effects on human and AI ratings. A significant interaction does not by itself show that one rating rises while the other falls. Joint marginal means determine that pattern. The $F\times P\times Q$ interaction tests whether the difference between benefiting and harmed positions changes this pattern.

Study 2 randomizes the final decision-maker within each payoff position. This randomization supports a causal interpretation of the record arrangement. The arrangement changes whether the human delegated and who made the final choice. It does not isolate decision control as a separate psychological mechanism. The comparison between benefiting and harmed records remains a position comparison because the two positions correspond to different payoff outcomes.

\subsection{Joint interpretation of responsibility ratings}

The joint movement of the human and AI ratings determines the classification as responsibility transfer, shared responsibility, or responsibility not fully taken up. The analysis will use preregistered marginal means, confidence intervals, and a smallest effect size of interest. Evidence of no meaningful change will require equivalence tests or a preregistered smallest effect size. A nonsignificant $p$-value is insufficient. The analysis compares the two ratings jointly and does not compare their simple sum across conditions. The ratings do not measure an objective total amount of responsibility.

\section{Expected Contributions}

The proposal examines conflict escalation and post-outcome responsibility judgments within a group-conflict process where AI delegation is available. Study 1 identifies the overall effect of making AI delegation available on escalation. Study 2 identifies the effect of the recorded delegation and final-decision arrangement on responsibility ratings. The two estimates address behavioral and attribution outcomes separately.

The proposal measures human and AI responsibility independently. The design can distinguish responsibility transfer, shared responsibility, and responsibility not fully taken up because the scale does not force one rating to rise when the other falls. The joint ratings show whether the transfer of final authority is accompanied by lower human responsibility or continued responsibility for both actors. Continued human responsibility is consistent with responsibility for authorization. This pattern alone does not identify that mechanism.

Study 1 collects responsibility judgments from members whose groups receive real payoff consequences. Study 2 tests whether the effect of the recorded decision arrangement differs between benefiting and harmed records. A claim about actual financial positions in Study 2 requires a specified and implemented link between the record and the evaluator's bonus.

The study does not include a human-agent condition, so it cannot establish whether an effect is unique to AI or applies to delegation more generally. Actual delegation in Study 1 is self-selected. Differences between delegators and non-delegators therefore do not identify the independent causal effect of delegation. The two studies also do not establish that post-outcome responsibility judgments caused earlier delegation or escalation.

\section{Ethics, Pilot Testing, and Preregistration}

The study will obtain ethics approval or an exemption determination before formal recruitment. Base compensation and performance bonuses will be separated. The lowest experimental outcome will not produce a negative payment. The ethics application and debriefing will disclose any use of pregenerated AI output, simulated opponents, or stored decision records.

Pilot testing will assess comprehension of the payoff matrix and responsibility items. It will also measure AI-delegation uptake, the escalation tendency of the fixed AI decision rule, and the frequency of asymmetric payoff outcomes. Study 1 and Study 2 will have separate simulation-based power analyses. Preregistration will fix the AI action rule, primary treatments, primary outcomes, clustering level, responsibility items, criteria for classifying responsibility patterns, and multiple-testing procedures.
