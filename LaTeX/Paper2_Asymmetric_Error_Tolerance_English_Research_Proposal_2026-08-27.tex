\section*{Abstract}

AI is often evaluated against a human alternative that also makes errors. Studies have found both lower and higher tolerance for algorithmic errors. This proposal uses a signed parameter, $\alpha$, to describe the AI acceptance threshold relative to an aggregate Human benchmark. Stage 1 tests whether $\alpha$ can be recovered from repeated choices and predicts held-out decisions. Stage 2 examines accuracy misperceptions and varies joint accuracy disclosure and real bonus consequences. Participants choose between their own answer and AI advice. Individual ability and confidence therefore enter the interpretation of the recovered threshold. The study seeks a comparable behavioral measure and tests explanations for variation in it.

\textbf{Keywords:} asymmetric error tolerance; AI adoption; Human counterfactual; source choice; behavioral parameter; performance misperception

\section{Research Background}

\subsection{Object of analysis}

The study examines individual choices between Human and AI performance of the same task. \Human{} denotes the aggregate performance of the relevant human population. In the experiment, this is estimated from previous participants. \AI{} denotes advice with researcher-controlled accuracy. Participants choose between their own answer and that advice.

The analysis concerns individual performance beliefs and source choices. The aggregate Human benchmark and individual ability are kept separate.

\subsection{The practical problem: compared with whom?}

An AI accuracy value needs a comparison point. The alternative may be a physician, driver, or manual reviewer. Acceptability depends on that alternative's performance and the consequences of error. Cost, review, and correction can also affect the comparison.

A stricter standard for AI may discourage its use when it performs better. A more lenient standard may favor AI when it performs worse. Comparing the two sources requires common tasks and performance metrics.

Different thresholds may be justified by differences in correlated failures, reviewability, responsibility, or the distribution of harm. The benchmark must account for relevant source differences.

\subsection{Theoretical background}

\subsubsection{Trust calibration as a normative boundary}

\citet{lee2004} describe appropriate reliance on automation as reliance suited to its capabilities. \citet{everett2026} distinguish actual trustworthiness, perceived trustworthiness, attitudinal trust, and trusting behavior. \citet{mehrotra2024} also distinguish beliefs, intentions, and actions.

User, system, and contextual factors predict trust in automation and AI~\citep{kaplan2023,schaefer2016}. The present experiment measures numerical accuracy beliefs and source choice. Its evidence therefore concerns performance evaluation and behavioral error tolerance. Claims about attitudinal trust would require additional measures.

\subsubsection{Performance beliefs and observable choice}

The analysis distinguishes objective accuracy, perceived accuracy, and choice. The values $q_a$ and $q_h$ describe task performance. Participants report their estimates as $\widehat q_{a,i}$ and $\widehat q_{h,i}$. They then choose whether to keep their answer or adopt the AI recommendation.

Accuracy judgments can be wrong for either source. Source choice can also reflect costs, convenience, and individual ability. The model examines performance misperception while allowing for these other explanations.

\subsubsection{Algorithm aversion, appreciation, and automation bias}

\citet{dietvorst2015} show that participants may lose confidence in an algorithm more quickly than in a human forecaster after observing both make errors, even when the algorithm performs better overall. \citet{logg2019} find that participants in several estimation and forecasting tasks may weight algorithmic advice more than human advice. Automation-bias research describes excessive adherence to automation despite warnings or automation errors~\citep{goddard2012}. Task subjectivity also changes the direction and magnitude of algorithm aversion~\citep{castelo2019}.

These studies report aversion, appreciation, and overreliance in different tasks. The proposed parameter allows either sign of the source difference.

\subsubsection{Acceptable error rates and minimum performance thresholds}

\citet{lenskjold2023} directly asked radiology employees about acceptable error rates for Human and AI reports and found lower average tolerance for AI errors. \citet{shariff2021} found that respondents may demand a safety threshold from autonomous vehicles that exceeds the threshold applied to human drivers. \citet{rebitschek2021} reported no uniform difference in error estimates or tolerance between algorithms and human experts. The direction therefore varies across studies.

Three explanations require separation: inaccurate performance beliefs, a fixed source preference, and different weights on errors. \citet{longoni2019} separated source, accuracy, and price utility in medical AI. A difference in the acceptable error rate alone can reflect several of these explanations.

\subsubsection{Evaluability and the Human benchmark}

The evaluability hypothesis holds that some attributes are difficult to interpret in separate evaluation because they lack a reference point; joint evaluation can change their evaluability and decision weight~\citep{hsee1996}. An isolated AI accuracy value may not tell a decision maker whether performance is sufficient. Presenting a Human performance benchmark measured on the same task and metric may make the evidence easier to interpret.

Joint disclosure may help participants compare the two sources against a common performance benchmark. Other source differences may remain.

\subsection{Literature gap}

Prior research documents differences in error tolerance across sources. The present study seeks a common signed measure of those differences. It first tests whether $\alpha$ can be recovered from choice thresholds. It then examines associations with accuracy misperceptions and responses to real error consequences.

\section{Research Questions}

The main research question is:

\begin{quote}
Why do people exhibit asymmetric error tolerance toward Human and AI errors?
\end{quote}

\condition{RQ1: Measurement.} Can $\alpha$ measure Asymmetric Error Tolerance from behavioral choice thresholds?

\condition{RQ2: Explanation.} How do errors in perceived Human and AI accuracy and the objective loss from an error explain the magnitude and direction of $\alpha$?

RQ1 establishes whether the proposed measure can be recovered reliably. RQ2 then examines variation in its magnitude and direction.

\section{Core Concepts and Variable Boundaries}

\subsection{Asymmetric Error Tolerance}

Asymmetric Error Tolerance is defined here as a difference in how equivalent Human and AI errors affect source choice when the task, objective performance metric, error consequences, and relevant costs are comparable.

Participants may require AI to exceed Human performance or accept it at lower performance. The sign of $\alpha$ records these two directions. Its magnitude records the deviation from the specified benchmark.

\subsection{Human counterfactual}

The Human counterfactual is the human source that would perform the task in the absence of AI. It is evaluated on the same population, task, time horizon, and performance metric.

The comparison requires matched error consequences, costs, review opportunities, and relevant source conditions. Remaining differences must enter the model or qualify its interpretation.

\section{Theoretical Framework}

\subsection{Notation and rational adoption rule}

A principal chooses between a Human agent $H$ and an AI system $A$. Define objective task accuracy as

\begin{equation*}
q_a=\text{objective AI task accuracy},\qquad
q_h=\text{objective aggregate Human task accuracy},
\end{equation*}

and objective error rates as

\begin{equation*}
e_a=1-q_a,\qquad e_h=1-q_h.
\end{equation*}

Let $L>0$ be the objective loss from an error, and let $c_h,c_a\geq0$ denote Human and AI deployment costs. For $j\in\{h,a\}$, expected total cost is

\begin{equation*}
C_j=e_jL+c_j.
\end{equation*}

A source-neutral rational decision maker chooses AI if and only if

\begin{equation*}
C_a<C_h,
\end{equation*}

or equivalently,

\begin{equation}
e_h-e_a>\frac{c_a-c_h}{L}.
\label{eq:rational-rule}
\end{equation}

Equation~\ref{eq:rational-rule} gives the source-neutral benchmark. The term $L$ denotes the consequence of one error.

\subsection{Model assumptions}

\condition{A1. Comparable alternatives.} Human and AI perform the same task and are evaluated with the same performance metric. The same type of error has the same objective consequence:

\begin{equation*}
L_h=L_a=L.
\end{equation*}

Any other source-specific difference that rationally affects choice must be matched or explicitly represented in $c_h$ and $c_a$.

\condition{A2. Risk neutrality.} The principal minimizes expected total cost.

\condition{A3. Independent errors.} Errors are independent draws.

\condition{A4. Costless evaluation.} The principal observes $e_h$ and $e_a$ accurately.

\condition{A5. Stationary environment.} The parameters $e_h,e_a,L,c_h,c_a$ remain fixed within each decision block.

These assumptions define the rational benchmark. They place no restriction on the sign of the estimated $\alpha$.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Representing Asymmetric Error Tolerance with $\alpha$}{Representing Asymmetric Error Tolerance with alpha}}

The framework introduces one departure from the objective benchmark. The behavioral weight on a Human error is normalized to one, and the behavioral weight on an AI error is $1+\alpha$:

\begin{equation*}
\widetilde L_h=L,\qquad \widetilde L_a=(1+\alpha)L.
\end{equation*}

Expected behavioral costs become

\begin{equation*}
\widetilde C_h=e_hL+c_h,
\end{equation*}

\begin{equation*}
\widetilde C_a=(1+\alpha)e_aL+c_a,
\qquad \alpha\in(-1,\infty).
\end{equation*}

The term $\widetilde L_a$ weights an AI error in the choice model. The objective consequence remains $L$. The choice rule becomes

\begin{equation}
e_h-e_a-\alpha e_a>\frac{c_a-c_h}{L}.
\label{eq:alpha-rule}
\end{equation}

Equation~\ref{eq:alpha-rule} gives the source-weighted comparison. The parameter has the following signed interpretation:

\begin{equation*}
\begin{cases}
\alpha>0, & \text{AI errors receive greater decision weight: excess intolerance},\\
\alpha=0, & \text{Human and AI errors receive source-neutral weight},\\
\alpha<0, & \text{AI errors receive lower decision weight: excess tolerance}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation*}

The parameter $\alpha$ is the relative AI error-weighting deviation. It describes the source difference in the model.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Identifying $\alpha$ from behavioral thresholds}{Identifying alpha from behavioral thresholds}}

Let $e_{a,i}^{*}$ denote the highest objective AI error rate that participant $i$ is willing to accept. At the indifference threshold,

\begin{equation*}
(1+\alpha_i)e_{a,i}^{*}L+c_a=e_hL+c_h.
\end{equation*}

Therefore,

\begin{equation}
\alpha_i=\frac{e_hL+c_h-c_a}{e_{a,i}^{*}L}-1.
\label{eq:alpha-recovery}
\end{equation}

Equation~\ref{eq:alpha-recovery} simplifies when $c_h=c_a$:

\begin{equation*}
\alpha_i=\frac{e_h}{e_{a,i}^{*}}-1.
\end{equation*}

The formula links the objective Human benchmark to an individual AI acceptance threshold. Stage 1 tests recovery, stability within condition, and prediction of held-out choices.

The estimate is relative to aggregate Human performance. Its structural interpretation requires a specified treatment of individual ability, item-level confidence, and fixed source preferences. That specification remains to be validated.

Recovery requires $L>0$, $e_{a,i}^{*}>0$, and a switching threshold in the observed range. Always accepting or rejecting AI provides a bound or leaves the threshold unidentified. Multiple accuracy levels help locate the threshold. Recovery simulations must also assess whether fixed preferences can be distinguished from error weights.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Stage 2: Explaining variation in $\alpha$}{Stage 2: Explaining variation in alpha}}

Stage 1 discloses $q_h$ and $q_a$ and verifies comprehension. Stage 2 crosses joint disclosure with the loss from an error, $L$. The information condition displays either both accuracies or neither. This produces the $2\times2$ design described below.

The analysis relates misperception magnitude $M_i^{A4}$ to $|\widehat\alpha_i|$ and relative misperception $R_i^{A4}$ to signed $\widehat\alpha_i$. The model also includes the objective error consequence $L$.

\begin{equation*}
\widehat q_{a,i}=\text{perceived AI task accuracy},\qquad
\widehat q_{h,i}=\text{perceived Human task accuracy}.
\end{equation*}

The signed judgment errors are

\begin{equation*}
G_{a,i}^{acc}=\widehat q_{a,i}-q_a,
\qquad
G_{h,i}^{acc}=\widehat q_{h,i}-q_h.
\end{equation*}

Their absolute components are

\begin{equation*}
M_{a,i}^{A4}=|G_{a,i}^{acc}|=|\widehat q_{a,i}-q_a|,
\qquad
M_{h,i}^{A4}=|G_{h,i}^{acc}|=|\widehat q_{h,i}-q_h|,
\end{equation*}

and total performance misperception is

\begin{equation}
M_i^{A4}=M_{a,i}^{A4}+M_{h,i}^{A4}
=|\widehat q_{a,i}-q_a|+|\widehat q_{h,i}-q_h|.
\label{eq:M}
\end{equation}

Equation~\ref{eq:M} adds absolute errors so that positive and negative mistakes cannot cancel. If total misperception is associated with $|\alpha|$, a component model compares the AI and Human error terms.

Direction is preserved with

\begin{equation}
R_i^{A4}=G_{a,i}^{acc}-G_{h,i}^{acc}
=(\widehat q_{a,i}-q_a)-(\widehat q_{h,i}-q_h).
\label{eq:R}
\end{equation}

In Equation~\ref{eq:R}, positive $R_i^{A4}$ indicates relative overestimation of AI. A negative value indicates relative underestimation. Zero means that the perceived accuracy difference matches the objective difference.

The total experimental effect on asymmetry magnitude is estimated as

\begin{equation}
|\widehat\alpha_i|
=\delta_0+\delta_1N_i^{Info}+\delta_2L_i^{High}
+\delta_3(N_i^{Info}L_i^{High})+u_i,
\label{eq:treatment}
\end{equation}

In Equation~\ref{eq:treatment}, $N_i^{Info}=1$ denotes disclosure of neither accuracy. The indicator $L_i^{High}=1$ denotes high stakes. A parallel model uses signed $\widehat\alpha_i$ to examine the direction of the treatment effect.

The magnitude model is

\begin{equation}
|\widehat\alpha_i|
=\gamma_0+\gamma_1M_i^{A4}+\gamma_2L_i
+\gamma_3M_i^{A4}L_i+\gamma_4\overline S_i+\varepsilon_i.
\label{eq:magnitude}
\end{equation}

Equation~\ref{eq:magnitude} estimates associations with asymmetry magnitude. The term $\overline S_i$ summarizes average confidence and may leave some item-level private information unaccounted for. The sign of $\gamma_1$ remains open.

If $M_i^{A4}$ is significantly associated with $|\widehat\alpha_i|$, the component model is

\begin{equation}
|\widehat\alpha_i|
=\kappa_0+\kappa_aM_{a,i}^{A4}+\kappa_hM_{h,i}^{A4}
+\kappa_2L_i+\kappa_3\overline S_i+\nu_i.
\label{eq:components}
\end{equation}

Equation~\ref{eq:components} compares the AI and Human error terms. Their relative associations will be assessed using standardized variables or a joint coefficient test.

The signed direction model is

\begin{equation}
\widehat\alpha_i
=\theta_0+\theta_1R_i^{A4}+\theta_2L_i
+\theta_3R_i^{A4}L_i+\theta_4\overline S_i+\xi_i.
\label{eq:direction}
\end{equation}

For Equation~\ref{eq:direction}, the proposed choice model predicts a lower $\alpha$ when AI is relatively overestimated. Thus $\theta_1<0$ is a candidate prediction for the signed model. The magnitude model has no corresponding sign restriction.

The misperception coefficients describe associations. Randomized disclosure identifies a treatment effect. A causal interpretation of measured $M$ and $R$ would require additional identification.

These estimates describe choice differences relative to the specified benchmark. A structural error-weight interpretation requires recovery, stability, and tests of competing explanations.

\section{Predictions and Explanatory Paths}

\subsection{Directional competition}

\condition{CP0: Source-neutral evaluation.} After objective performance, error consequences, and deployment costs are matched, $\alpha=0$. Human-AI choice differences are explained by objective conditions.

\condition{CP1: Excess intolerance.} Under comparable conditions, $\alpha>0$. AI errors receive greater decision weight than equivalent Human errors and may produce under-adoption.

\condition{CP2: Excess tolerance.} Under comparable conditions, $\alpha<0$. AI errors receive lower decision weight than equivalent Human errors and may produce over-adoption.

\subsection{A4 checks and structural interpretation}

\condition{A4.} Positive $M_i^{A4}$ indicates misperception of at least one accuracy. Nonzero $R_i^{A4}$ indicates an incorrect relative comparison. These errors can contribute to the recovered threshold deviation.

\condition{Residual differences.} A stable nonzero $\alpha$ with accurate performance beliefs calls for checks of A1, A2, A3, and A5. These checks assess competing explanations before interpreting the deviation as a source-dependent error weight.

\subsection{Recovery and behavioral meaning}

The slot-machine task provides controlled probabilities. A useful $\alpha$ should be recoverable across performance levels, stable within condition, and predictive of held-out choices. Recovery must account for fixed source preferences and individual ability.

\subsection{Misperception and stakes}

The effect of $L$ depends on the cost structure. With $c_h=c_a$, $L$ cancels from the simplest threshold rule. A difference across stake conditions must be assessed against that benchmark. Such a difference may reflect attention, effort, or risk responses.

The models compare total, component, and relative accuracy errors with the recovered $\alpha$. Differences in recovery precision across conditions will be assessed before interpreting treatment effects.

\subsection{Benchmark prediction}

Under the evaluability hypothesis, jointly displaying Human and AI accuracy for the same task and metric should improve the evaluability of performance evidence. The proposal therefore predicts:

\begin{quote}
Compared with disclosing neither accuracy, jointly disclosing comparable Human and AI accuracy will reduce $|\alpha|$.
\end{quote}

Disclosure is predicted to reduce the distance from zero. AI adoption may rise or fall, depending on the initial direction of the deviation.

\section{Research Method}

\subsection{General design}

The experiment uses three slot machines in each decision block. Researchers fix their winning probabilities and generate the outcome sequence before the experiment. Participants see only past outcomes.

Participants first complete four or five observation or practice rounds. They inspect the histories, make judgments, observe AI advice, and see the outcomes. They then make one consequential bet. Before seeing AI advice, they choose a machine and report confidence $S_{it}$. They then decide whether to keep their answer or adopt the AI recommendation. The result changes their real experimental bonus.

The pretest benchmark $q_h$ is described as the average accuracy of previous participants. AI accuracy $q_a$ is controlled through pregenerated advice. Independent blocks vary $q_a$ to locate an acceptance threshold. Estimating individual $\alpha$ requires choices across several accuracy levels.

Stage 1 evaluates recovery under joint disclosure and low stakes. Stage 2 crosses disclosure with stakes. Whether the stages use separate samples or an analysis sequence within one factorial study remains unresolved. Disclosure is assigned between participants. Previously disclosed benchmarks cannot later be treated as unknown. Stakes may vary within or between participants; this allocation remains to be fixed. Within-participant variation would require condition-specific thresholds and analysis of dependent observations.

\subsection{Parameters to estimate}

The control condition estimates $\alpha$ from choices across AI-accuracy levels. The analysis reports its distribution and the proportions above, near, and below zero. It then estimates treatment differences in magnitude and direction. Reporting the distribution avoids masking opposite individual deviations in an average near zero.

Stage 2 uses $|\widehat\alpha_i|$ and $\widehat\alpha_i$ as outcomes. Threshold-estimation uncertainty must enter both analyses. Absolute values can turn noise near zero into apparent asymmetry, and precision may vary across conditions. Recovery simulations will quantify these effects. A null association with total misperception also leaves possible component-specific associations unresolved.

\subsection{Pilot testing and ethics}

Pilot testing will assess comprehension and recovery of switching thresholds. Four or five observation rounds may provide too little information in the undisclosed conditions. The number of rounds will be adjusted if recovery is unreliable. Sample sizes will follow recovery simulations, pilot distributions, and a prespecified minimum effect of interest.

Human-participant research will obtain ethics approval and informed consent before data collection. Materials will explain the real bonus consequences. Any later medical or driving scenarios will be identified as research materials. Primary measures, exclusions, and analyses will be preregistered. Materials, anonymized data, and code will be shared where consent and privacy permit.

\section{Expected Contributions}

The proposed measure places greater and lower tolerance for AI errors on one signed scale. Its value depends on recovery from behavior and prediction of held-out choices. Successful validation would support comparison of threshold deviations across conditions with comparable tasks and benchmarks.

The explanatory analysis separates objective accuracy, perceived accuracy, and source choice. It examines whether total misperception is associated with the magnitude of $\alpha$ and whether relative misperception is associated with its direction. Randomized disclosure and stakes provide additional tests of how information and error consequences change the recovered measure.

The findings could inform comparative performance disclosure. Their immediate scope is individual choice in the experimental task. Extensions to other domains, organizational adoption, or regulation require corresponding evidence. An effect unique to AI would also require comparison with a rules-based algorithm.
