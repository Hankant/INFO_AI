## Slide 2 — Research background

### Speaker script

When Human and AI performance is similar, people do not always judge their errors in the same way.

In areas such as automated driving and medical AI, an AI error may receive extra attention. People may reject the AI even when its overall performance is better than the Human benchmark. This pattern is often discussed as algorithm aversion.

The opposite pattern also exists. In some tasks, people continue to follow AI advice even after the AI makes mistakes. This may appear as algorithm appreciation or automation bias.

So the direction is not stable. People may be too strict with AI in one setting and too tolerant in another. This is the puzzle we want to explain.

## Slide 3 — Research gap

### Speaker script

These studies are examining the same broad practical problem: how people respond to AI errors compared with Human errors.

However, their findings are difficult to discuss together. Each literature describes the problem within a narrow task or decision setting. They also use different outcomes. As a result, we still lack a common way to measure the Human–AI difference.

That difference may come from several sources. People may misunderstand Human or AI performance. Their choices may also reflect objective conditions, such as error losses or deployment costs. After these factors are considered, there may still be a preference for or against the source itself.

Our approach is therefore simple. We first measure the overall difference. We then examine where it comes from.

## Slide 4 — Core concept and research questions

### Speaker script

Our central question is: why do people tolerate equivalent Human and AI errors differently?

We call this overall phenomenon Asymmetric Error Tolerance. It means that equivalent Human and AI errors lead to different choices under otherwise comparable conditions.

The advantage of this concept is its broad scope. It can include the subjective and objective factors we have just discussed. It does not assume that every difference comes from trust alone.

Now suppose that one parameter, alpha, can measure this asymmetry. We can first estimate alpha from behaviour. We can then study which factors change it. In this way, different explanations can be tested within one framework.

This leads to two questions. Can alpha measure Asymmetric Error Tolerance? And what factors increase or reduce alpha?

## Slide 5 — Rational benchmark

### Speaker script

We first need a rational benchmark.

A decision-maker chooses between a Human source and an AI system. Each source has an objective error rate, shown by e. An error produces a loss, L. Each source also has a deployment cost, c.

Expected total cost is therefore the error rate times the loss from an error, plus the deployment cost.

The rule at the bottom shows when the decision-maker should choose AI. AI should be selected when its advantage in error rate is large enough to offset its additional deployment cost.

The assumptions on the right define this benchmark. The two options are comparable. The decision-maker is risk neutral. Errors are independent. Error rates are observed accurately. The environment remains stable within each decision block.

These assumptions are not descriptions of real behaviour. They give us a clear point of comparison.

## Slide 6 — Introducing alpha

### Speaker script

We now introduce one departure from the benchmark.

The weight on a Human error remains L. The weight on an AI error becomes one plus alpha, multiplied by L. Everything else stays the same.

This gives alpha a direct interpretation. If alpha equals zero, equivalent Human and AI errors receive the same weight. If alpha is positive, AI errors receive more weight. This is excess intolerance. If alpha is negative, AI errors receive less weight. This is excess tolerance.

Alpha therefore places the two opposite patterns on one signed scale.

At this stage, alpha is a behavioural parameter. It summarizes the departure shown in participants' choices. The second stage examines what produces this difference.

## Slide 7 — Explaining why alpha varies

### Speaker script

After alpha is estimated, it becomes the dependent variable in the second stage.

We examine two main explanations.

The first is performance misperception. Participants may misjudge AI accuracy, Human accuracy, or both. We add the two absolute estimation errors to measure total misperception. We first test whether it explains the magnitude of alpha. If it does, we compare the AI and Human components. We also retain a signed relative-error measure to explain the direction of alpha.

The second explanation is the objective loss from an error, or the stakes. We test whether alpha changes when the same error produces a larger consequence.

We also measure perceived trustworthiness separately. This asks whether participants see the AI as worthy of trust. It is different from asking them to estimate a numerical accuracy rate.

The second stage therefore separates misperception magnitude, misperception direction, trust evaluation, and stakes.

## Slide 8 — Experimental task

### Speaker script

We use a controlled slot-machine task to measure the AI acceptance threshold.

Participants see three abstract slot machines. Their winning probabilities and outcome sequences are controlled by the researchers.

The task has four steps. First, participants observe four or five practice rounds. Second, they choose the machine they think will win and report their confidence. Third, they see the AI's recommendation. Finally, they decide whether to keep their own answer or adopt the AI recommendation. This final choice affects their real bonus.

Human accuracy comes from an independent pretest. AI accuracy is controlled by the researchers. In the disclosed conditions, participants see both values. In the non-disclosed conditions, they see neither value.

Across independent blocks, we vary the objective accuracy of the AI. This allows us to find the highest AI error rate that a participant will still accept. That threshold is then used to estimate alpha.

One choice is not enough. We need several AI accuracy levels to locate the threshold.

## Slide 9 — Two-by-two design

### Speaker script

The experiment uses a two-by-two design.

The first factor is comparative accuracy information. In one condition, both Human and AI accuracy are disclosed. In the other condition, neither accuracy is disclosed. This allows us to test whether participants misjudge Human performance, AI performance, or both.

The second factor is stakes. Participants make the same type of choice under either low or high error consequences. This tests whether a larger loss changes alpha.

The four cells let us estimate each effect and their joint effect. The disclosed-information and low-stakes condition provides the baseline.

Other features remain constant. The objective Human and AI accuracy values, slot-machine probabilities, outcome sequences, and AI accuracy levels are matched across conditions. Every final choice has a real bonus consequence.

## Slide 10 — Expected contributions

### Speaker script

The study is expected to make two types of contribution.

For behavioural decision and measurement research, alpha provides one signed scale. It covers excess intolerance, source neutrality, and excess tolerance. The study also separates three questions: how alpha is defined, whether it can be measured from choice thresholds, and whether it predicts later choices. The benchmark assumptions then become empirical checks instead of hidden assumptions.

For AI trust research, the study keeps several concepts separate. These include objective task performance, perceived task accuracy, perceived trustworthiness, trust attitude, and source choice. This lets us test whether asymmetric choice comes from ability misperception, broader trust evaluation, or non-trust choice factors.

The main goal is not to label every AI choice as trust. The goal is to measure the behavioural difference first and then explain its sources carefully.
