## Slide 1 — Responsibility Attribution in Intergroup Conflict

Today I will introduce my third research proposal. It studies what happens when AI becomes a decision agent in intergroup conflict. I ask whether access to AI delegation changes conflict escalation. I also examine how responsibility is divided between the human and AI after the outcome.

---

## Slide 2 — Claude has reportedly been used in U.S. military operations related to Iran

AI is likely to play a growing role in large-scale conflicts. Even when a human gives formal approval, AI may shape or make an important decision. This creates a responsibility dispute. Does bringing AI into the decision process change conflict escalation? And after the outcome, how will people divide responsibility between the human and AI?

---

## Slide 3 — Two literatures explain different stages of the same decision process

Two research streams examine AI agency from different angles. AI delegation research asks when people give decision authority to AI and whether this changes behavior. AI responsibility-attribution research asks how people divide responsibility between humans and AI after an outcome. Together, these studies show that AI can affect both who makes a decision and who is held responsible.

---

## Slide 4 — AI involvement does not move responsibility in one fixed direction

Existing findings do not point in one direction. Some studies find that AI receives responsibility for a harmful outcome. Other studies find that responsibility remains with the human or the organization. Human blame may even increase when the person could have intervened. These mixed findings suggest that the AI label alone cannot explain responsibility judgments. We need to know which parts of the decision remained under human control and which parts were assigned to AI.

---

## Slide 5 — Evaluator position may change how the same decision record is interpreted

Earlier research shows that moral judgment can depend on the evaluator's position. Schiller and colleagues found group bias in costly punishment. Dong and Bocian showed that self-interest can change how people divide responsibility between a human and AI. However, we still lack evidence from an AI-delegated conflict in which some participants actually gain and others actually lose. My experiment creates these payoff positions and asks whether they judge the same decision differently.

---

## Slide 6 — Existing studies separate stages that occur together in real conflicts

In a real conflict, delegation, action, consequences, and responsibility judgments occur in one sequence. A human first decides whether to hand over the final choice. The human or AI then acts. The outcome creates winners and losers. Only then do the affected people judge responsibility. Existing studies usually isolate one part of this process, often through questionnaires or hypothetical scenarios. The missing evidence is a study that observes these stages in the same intergroup setting with real payoff consequences.

---

## Slide 7 — The study asks three questions within one conflict process

The main question is whether introducing AI as a decision agent changes conflict escalation and the later allocation of responsibility within the group. I divide this into three testable questions. First, does access to AI delegation change people's willingness to delegate? Second, does this option change the final escalation outcome? Third, after the same type of outcome, do people who gain, people who lose, and the decision-maker allocate responsibility differently between the human and AI? The design does not assume that people delegate in order to avoid responsibility.

---

## Slide 8 — Responsibility judgments combine obligation, control, and causal contribution

To explain the responsibility judgment, I distinguish three bases drawn from responsibility and blame research. The first is role obligation. It asks who had a duty to make the decision carefully and protect the group. In this experiment, that duty is attached to the human decision-maker's role. The second is decision control. It asks who could select, change, or stop the final action. In the Baseline condition, the human has final control. After delegation, AI has final-action control, while the human still made the authorization decision. The third is causal contribution. It asks whose action most directly produced the group's Hawk or Dove choice. The human contributes directly in the Baseline condition. After delegation, the human enables the action, and AI makes the final choice. These three labels are analytical categories derived from prior theory. They are not three randomized treatments, and they are not presented as an established O-C-K model.

---

## Slide 9 — Delegation can produce three distinct responsibility configurations

Separating obligation, control, and causal contribution allows three empirical patterns. Responsibility transfer means that lower human responsibility is matched by higher AI responsibility. Responsibility accumulation means that AI receives responsibility while human responsibility remains high. Responsibility dilution means that human responsibility falls without an equal increase in AI responsibility. These are possible patterns in the data. The theoretical test is whether responsibility follows final control alone, or whether the human's continuing role obligation keeps responsibility attached to the human after delegation.

---

## Slide 10 — One experiment follows the conflict from delegation to attribution

This is one continuous group experiment. Participants first receive or do not receive access to AI delegation. Then the final conflict choices produce gains and losses. The same participants evaluate responsibility after seeing the outcome. Random assignment identifies whether access to AI delegation changes escalation. Actual delegation is chosen by the human, so attribution differences by final decision-maker are conditional comparisons. I do not claim that later responsibility judgments caused the earlier choice.

---

## Slide 11 — Real group stakes create meaningful beneficiary and harmed-party positions

The group design is supported by social-preference research. Inequity aversion tells us that people care about payoff differences. Intention-based reciprocity tells us that perceived intentions and available choices can also matter. In this study, these ideas justify using real group payoffs. The goal is to create meaningful beneficiary and harmed-party positions. Social preferences are not treated as a mediator of responsibility shifting.

---

## Slide 12 — The group design adds social stakes without giving members sanctioning power

The group members face real consequences from the conflict choice. Some members benefit, while others are harmed. They then evaluate the responsibility of the human and AI. However, they cannot directly reduce the opposing decision-maker’s payoff. This boundary is important. It keeps responsibility attribution separate from punishment, retaliation, or counteraction.

---

## Slide 13 — The main experiment randomizes access to AI delegation

Participants form two opposing groups. Each group has one decision-maker and several members. In the Baseline condition, the human makes the final choice. In AI-D, the human may decide or delegate to AI. If the human delegates, the AI choice becomes final. The Hawk–Dove choice remains simultaneous and opaque. Neither the human nor AI sees the opponent's current choice or private information. The main result comes from the first-round comparison between Baseline and AI-D. Actual delegation is self-selected, so it is reported as a process measure.

---

## Slide 14 — Responsibility is assessed after the same realized conflict

After the conflict, participants see the actual authorization record, both final actions, and the payoff outcome. Beneficiaries and harmed parties then evaluate the human and AI. The decision-maker's self-evaluation is an additional comparison. I measure causal responsibility and moral blame separately for the human and AI. Because the human chooses whether to delegate, realized AI authority is not randomly assigned. These attribution comparisons are therefore conditional. A separate matched-record experiment would be needed to make a causal claim about final authority.

---

## Slide 15 — The design can make three bounded contributions

The study can make three contributions if the proposed patterns are supported. First, it can show what happens when the bases of responsibility are divided between a human and AI. This helps distinguish transfer, accumulation, and dilution. Second, it tests motivated responsibility attribution with real group stakes. Third, it studies escalation and later attribution within one conflict sequence.

The claims remain limited. The study does not compare AI delegation with human delegation. It also does not show that responsibility expectations cause escalation.
