## Slide 8 — Separate ratings reveal how responsibility is divided

Now I turn to the measurement of responsibility. Each participant gives two separate ratings from zero to one hundred. One rating is for the group's human decision-maker. The other is for the group's AI agent. We do not force the two ratings to add up to one hundred. This is important because people may hold both actors responsible at the same time. We then examine the two ratings together. A lower human rating and a higher AI rating indicate responsibility transfer. High ratings for both actors indicate shared responsibility. A lower human rating without a clear increase for AI means that responsibility is not fully taken up. These are descriptive patterns used to interpret the results. They are not three established psychological constructs.

## Slide 9 — Study 1 compares baseline decisions with voluntary, binding AI delegation

Study 1 tests what happens when AI delegation becomes available. Participants are randomly assigned to one of two conditions. In the baseline condition, there is no AI delegation option. The designated human must make the final conflict choice. In the AI-D condition, the human can keep final authority or delegate the final choice to AI. This is pure and binding delegation. Once the person delegates, the AI choice takes effect directly. The person cannot preview, revise, veto, withdraw, or reconfirm it. The conflict itself remains opaque. Both groups choose at the same time, and neither the human nor AI can see the opponent's current choice or private information. We record actual delegation within the AI-D condition. We compare final escalation between the two randomized conditions.

## Slide 10 — Group payoffs create real positions for responsibility judgments

This slide explains why the experiment uses groups. Each group contains one decision-maker and two members without decision authority. All three members share the payoff produced by the conflict. The decision-maker therefore makes a choice that affects other real participants, not only the self. This creates social stakes and makes the conflict closer to organizational and political decisions. The two groups choose at the same time, and their choices jointly determine the outcome. After the outcome, one side may benefit while the other side may be harmed. Each side then evaluates its own human decision-maker and its own AI agent. The members do not punish the opposing decision-maker. Their role is to judge responsibility from a real interest position.

## Slide 11 — Two studies separate the causal and conditional comparisons

The two studies answer different causal questions. Study 1 randomizes whether the AI-D option is available. It therefore identifies the overall effect of making AI delegation available on final escalation. This is the intention-to-treat effect. Actual delegation is chosen by the participant, so it is not a randomized treatment. The final payoff position is also produced by the two groups' choices. Comparisons involving delegation or payoff position in Study 1 are therefore conditional. Study 2 addresses the responsibility question more directly. It uses matched conflict records. Within a given payoff position, the rules, both actions, and the outcome are held fixed. The only randomized difference is whether the record says that the human made the final choice or delegated the same choice to AI. This identifies how recorded final authority changes the two responsibility ratings.

## Slide 12 — The proposal makes three bounded contributions

Finally, the proposal has three expected contributions. First, it estimates whether access to a voluntary AI decision agent changes conflict escalation. Actual delegation is reported separately, so the randomized treatment is not confused with the participant's later choice. Second, the study measures human and AI responsibility independently. This allows us to distinguish simple responsibility transfer, shared responsibility, and responsibility that is not fully taken up. Third, the study places responsibility judgments inside a conflict with real group payoffs. It examines whether benefiting and harmed groups form different human–AI responsibility profiles. The matched-record study then isolates the effect of final authority within each payoff position. The claims remain limited. Without a human-delegation condition, the study cannot show that the effect is unique to AI. It also does not establish responsibility as a causal mediator of delegation or escalation.
